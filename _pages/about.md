My name is Jungmin Han (한정민, 韩政潣, or 韓政潣). I am currently a postdoctoral researcher for [Patterns of Conflict Emergence](https://forecastlab.org) project at [Trinity College Dublin](https://www.tcd.ie/Political_Science/). Also, I am an affiliated faculty member at the [Ford Institute of Human Secruty](https://www.fordinstitute.pitt.edu) at the University of Pittsburgh. My research and teaching interests center on international security, conflict resolution, foreign policy, and political behavior, with a regional focus on East Asia. 

I earned my Ph.D. degree in Political Science at the [University of Pittsburgh](https://www.polisci.pitt.edu) with a focus on International Relations and Research Methods. Prior to this, I completed my MA in International Relations from [Peking University](https://www.sis.pku.edu.cn/en/) in Beijing, China, as well as a double-BA degree in Political Science and Economics from [State University of New York at Stony Brook](https://www.stonybrook.edu) and in Public Administration from the [University of Seoul](https://www.uos.ac.kr/eng/web/main).

The central motivation of [my research](/research/) is to advance understanding of _why the public resists peace despite its leader’s successful cooperation with a foreign adversary_. Even when democratic leaders successfully elicit cooperation from rival states, many citizens continue to view them negatively and resist conciliatory policies. My research, including my doctoral dissertation, investigates the socio-psychological barriers that sustain such public resistance to peace in East Asia. Along with this theme, [published works](/publications/) at _Conflict Management and Peace Science_ and _Journal of Contemporary China_ show how identity-based biases shape public interpretations of rivals’ cooperative gestures, often neutralizing or even reversing their peace-promoting effects. Also, reflecting my broader interests in political behavior, democracy, and computational text analysis, my work at _European Journal of Political Research_ examines how greater online visibility disproportionately exposes female candidates to multiple forms of online violence in in Türkiye.

Furthermore, I have several [working projects](/research/) on the impact of more broader political contexts on public opinion on peace, foreign intervention in civil wars, and online violence during electoral campaigns.
